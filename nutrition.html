Nutrition and Recovery

As a joint replacement surgeon, one of the most common questions I hear is: “What can I do before surgery to make my recovery smoother?” The good news is that there is a lot you can do—long before you arrive in the operating room—to support your healing, strengthen your body, and improve your overall well being.

Think of this period as preparing the soil before planting a garden. The healthier the foundation, the better the outcome.

Below are evidence informed, practical steps you can take to support your body before surgery. These are general principles—not personalized medical advice—and your own healthcare team can help tailor recommendations to your individual needs.

1. Nourish Your Body Before Surgery

Food as a Building Block for Healing

After joint replacement, your body is busy rebuilding and repairing soft tissues to restore strength. Eating well before surgery gives your body the raw materials it needs for that work.

Healthy sources of protein and fiber are especially important. Protein supports tissue repair, and fiber helps maintain gut health, stabilize blood sugar, and prevent constipation—something many patients struggle with after surgery.

You may have heard the phrase "food is medicine." While not every food acts like a medication, certain eating patterns can have powerful effects on metabolism, inflammation, and long-term health. For example:

  • Some foods naturally stimulate GLP-1 pathways, which help regulate appetite and blood sugar.
    (How to activate GLP-1 naturally |Ohio State Health & Discovery)
  • Certain dietary patterns are associated with reduced cancer risk and recurrence.
    (Vitamin D, Omega-3 Fatty Acids, and a Simple Home Exercise Program May Reduce Cancer Risk |Frontiers in Aging, Nutrition and Physical Activity Guideline for Cancer Survivors | American Cancer Society)
  • In some cases, specific eating approaches can even play a role in cancer treatment.
    (Effect of fasting on cancer |Cancer Science)

Change can feel overwhelming, but remember: every meal is an opportunity to help your body heal.

If you want individualized guidance, a registered dietitian can help you choose foods and patterns of eating that align with your goals—whether that’s weight loss, longevity, or simply eating more intentionally.

2. Key Nutrition Targets During Recovery

While everyone’s needs differ, research suggests that during rehabilitation, many people benefit from:

  • Protein: approximately 1.6–2.0 g per kg of body weight per day, ideally in 20–40 g servings spaced throughout the day.
    (Pre- and Post-Surgical Nutrition for Preservation of Muscle Mass, Strength, and Functionality Following Orthopedic Surgery | Nutrients)
  • Fiber: 25–35 g per day from whole foods to support digestion, weight stability, and blood glucose control.

These are general ranges from published research—not personalized targets—and your healthcare team can help you understand what’s appropriate for you.

3. Eating Patterns That Support Healing and Longevity

There is no single “joint replacement diet.” In fact, I don’t love the word diet at all—it implies something temporary. What we’re really talking about is adopting a sustainable lifestyle that supports your health.

Here are several evidence supported eating patterns that can be adapted to your needs:

Mediterranean-Style Eating

  • Emphasizes vegetables, fruits, legumes, whole grains, fish, and healthy fats
  • Flexible, sustainable, and widely studied
  • Associated with reduced inflammation and improved cardiovascular health
  • Example resource: Mediterranean Diet: Food List & Meal Plan

Portfolio Eating Pattern (for high cholesterol)

  • Focuses on plant-based proteins (~50 g/day)
  • Minimizes saturated fats
  • Allows fish, lean poultry, and low-fat dairy
  • Designed to lower LDL cholesterol and ApoB
  • Example resource: Plant Performance

DASH Eating Pattern (for high blood pressure)

  • Rich in magnesium and potassium
  • Encourages fruits, vegetables, whole grains, lean proteins
  • Reduces sodium intake (though response varies)
  • Example resource: A Week With the DASH Eating Plan

Smart Carbohydrate Approach (for diabetes or prediabetes)

  • Prioritizes high-quality carbohydrates (quinoa, buckwheat, chickpeas, sweet potatoes)
  • Uses retrogradation (cook, cool, reheat) to increase resistant starch
  • Balances carbs with protein, fiber, and healthy fats
  • Example resources: Diet sheet of what to eat - Dr. David Unwin, A low carb diet sheet for type 2 diabetes - Dr David Unwin

Across all these patterns, the themes are the same: whole foods, high protein, high fiber, healthy fats, and minimizing highly processed foods.

4. If Weight Loss Is One of Your Goals

Not everyone needs or wants to lose weight before surgery, but if it’s part of your goals, here are helpful principles:

  • Weight changes are driven primarily by nutrition, though exercise supports muscle maintenance and sets a foundation for metabolic health.
  • Aim for highprotein meals (often 30 g or more of clean, high quality protein per meal) and highfiberfoods. Avoid highly processed proteins and simple/refined grains.
  • Cutting out alcohol and decreasing added/free sugar is one of the most effective first steps
  • Keep in mind that BMI and scale weight alone can be imperfect measures of progress. A far more accurate indicator of metabolic health is your waist-to-height ratio. Keeping your waist circumference less than half your height is strongly associated with lower risk of cardiovascular disease and diabetes. So if you’ve spent weeks improving your nutrition and movement patterns and the scale hasn’t changed much — but your body looks and feels different — take that as a positive sign. You’ve likely reduced visceral fat and gained muscle, both of which meaningfully improve your longterm health.

Why Alcohol Works Against Your Goals

Sharing a drink is part of many social and cultural traditions, and it’s understandable that alcohol may not be completely avoidable. That said, if your goal is longterm health and getting the most longevity out of your joint replacement, minimizing alcohol is one of the most meaningful choices you can make. Alcohol disrupts deep, restorative sleep, increases inflammation, interferes with muscle maintenance, and raises blood sugar by altering liver metabolism. It also provides calories without nutrients and is classified as a Group 1 carcinogen and there is no truly safe level of consumption from a cancer risk standpoint.

If you choose to drink, save it for special occasions rather than making it a daily or weekly part of your diet. Limiting alcohol supports better sleep, stronger muscles, and a healthier, longerlasting outcome.

Alcohol:

  • Raises estrogen levels, which can affect muscle maintenance
  • Raises blood sugar by interfering with liver metabolism
  • Increases inflammation
  • Is a Class I carcinogen (same category as tobacco and asbestos) (Known and Probable Human Carcinogens | American Cancer Society)
  • Has no “safe” level of consumption from a cancer risk standpoint ( No level of alcohol consumption is safe for our health | World Health Organization, The risks of drinking too much | National Health Service, Alcohol Fact Sheet | World Health Organization)

Cutting Out Added Sugar

Reducing added sugar is one of the simplest and most powerful steps you can take to improve your health before surgery. It’s not about eliminating joy from eating — it’s about being intentional and understanding where sugar hides

Dessert every now and then is perfectly normal and enjoyable. In fact, a small amount of sugar paired with a high protein meal or high fiber snack can fit into a healthy diet. The American Heart Association recommends keeping added sugar below 25–36 grams per day, yet the average American consumes more than twice that amount

The real challenge isn’t the occasional dessert — it’s the hidden sugars in everyday foods. Many packaged items contain added sugars even when they don’t taste sweet. Common culprits include:

  • Ketchup and jarred pasta sauces
  • Packaged breads and granola
  • Flavored yogurt and oatmeal
  • Pre made salad dressings and marinades

When reading labels, check the first three ingredients. If any form of sugar appears there, consider that food a dessert in disguise. Added sugars can masquerade under many names, such as dextrose, glucose, fructose, maltose, sucrose, brown rice syrup, corn syrup, malt syrup, or evaporated cane juice.

Also be mindful of “free sugars” — like fruit juices — which can spike blood sugar just as much as soda. Whole fruits, on the other hand, contain fiber that slows absorption and supports gut health.

By cutting back on added sugars and focusing on whole, minimally processed foods, you’ll help stabilize blood glucose, reduce inflammation, and give your body a stronger foundation for healing.

5. Build and Maintain Muscle Before Surgery

Muscle mass is one of your greatest assets going into joint replacement.

  • Pre operative range of motion (ROM) strongly predicts post operative ROM, especially for knee replacements
  • Strength training counters age-related muscle loss
  • If weights feel intimidating, start with body weight exercises
  • If exercise feels overwhelming, begin with a 5–10 minute walk after each meal, working toward 30 minutes of daily walking

6. Prioritize Sleep—Your Recovery Depends on It

Sleep is one of the most underrated tools for healing. If sleep is difficult before surgery, it may remain challenging afterward, so establishing good habits now can help.

General strategies many people find helpful include:

  • Avoid screens 1–2 hours before bed or use blue light filters
  • Keep caffeine earlier in the day
  • Avoid alcohol at night—it disrupts deep, restorative sleep
  • Try an acupressure mat to calm the nervous system
  • Use white noise
  • Listen to guided sleep meditations or calming podcasts
  • Some people explore magnesium glycinate supplements, though this is not appropriate for everyone and should be discussed with a healthcare professional

Final Thoughts

Preparing for joint replacement is not just about the day of surgery—it’s about supporting your whole body in the weeks and months beforehand. Nutrition, movement, sleep, and lifestyle choices all play a role in how you feel going into surgery and how you recover afterward.

Every positive change you make now is an investment in your future mobility, comfort, and independence.
